<p class="lead-answer">Speaking up in meetings is a habit, not a talent. Prepare one point before the meeting, give yourself ten seconds to start when the urge comes, and build on someone else's point if starting cold feels hard. Speak once per meeting for two weeks — confidence arrives because you spoke, not before.</p>

You know the feeling. You have a good point, you rehearse it in your head, you wait for the right moment — and then someone else says it, or the discussion moves on. Afterwards you replay the meeting and feel frustrated with yourself.

Speaking up is not a talent. It is a habit, and habits can be built with small, repeatable steps.

## Why we freeze

Most people who stay quiet in meetings are not short of ideas. They are running a quick risk calculation: *What if I sound stupid? What if I am wrong? What if I interrupt?* That calculation happens in a second, and silence usually wins because it feels safe — that freeze is your body's [stress response](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/stress), trying to protect you, not sabotage you. What psychologists call [assertiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assertiveness) — expressing your views honestly while respecting others — is a skill, not a trait, and it can be practised like any other.

The problem is that silence has a cost too. Your work becomes invisible, decisions get made without your input, and over time you start to believe your ideas are not worth hearing. They are.

## 1. Prepare one point, not a speech

Before the meeting, write down **one** thing you want to say. Not a script — just a sentence or two. "The launch date is risky because the testing window is only two days." Having it written down removes the hardest part: finding the words in the moment.

If you have nothing prepared, listen for the first five minutes, then contribute a question. "Can we go back to the timeline for a minute?" Questions count as speaking up, and they are often easier than statements.

## 2. Use the ten-second rule

When you feel the urge to speak, give yourself ten seconds to start. Not ten minutes of rehearsing — ten seconds. Raise your hand, unmute, or simply begin: "I would like to add something here."

The first words are the hardest. Once you are talking, the rest usually follows. And here is the secret most confident speakers know: nobody remembers a slightly clumsy opening. They remember the point.

## 3. Build on someone else's point

You do not always need an original idea. Adding to what someone said is a perfectly good way in: "Building on what Priya said, I think the risk is bigger in the second phase." This is easier than starting cold, it shows you are listening, and it puts your voice in the room.

## 4. If you get interrupted, return calmly

It will happen. When it does, do not apologise and disappear. Wait for a pause and say, "I would like to finish my point." Calm and direct beats loud every time. Most people will respect it — and the ones who do not were never going to listen to an apology either.

## Practice where the stakes are low

Start in smaller meetings, or with people you trust. Speak once per meeting for two weeks. That is the whole assignment. Confidence does not arrive before you speak; it arrives because you spoke, survived, and nothing terrible happened.
