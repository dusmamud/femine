<p class="lead-answer">Feeling stuck in your career is a signal, not a sentence. First name what is actually missing — boredom, no growth, wrong values, or no recognition — then run a two-week experiment, talk to someone who made a similar move, and write a 90-day plan with one skill, one project and one person.</p>

Stuck does not usually look dramatic. You are not failing — you have a decent job, people think you are doing fine. But Sunday evenings feel heavy, Monday mornings feel grey, and somewhere in the back of your mind there is a question you keep postponing: *is this it?*

That heavy Sunday feeling is often [stress](https://www.apa.org/topics/stress) — your body telling you something needs attention, not laziness.

That feeling is not ingratitude. It is information. Here is what to do with it.

## Step 1: name what is actually missing

"Stuck" is too vague to fix. Get specific. Which of these is true?

- **Boredom** — the work is too easy, or too repetitive.
- **No growth** — you have not learned anything new in a year.
- **Wrong values** — the work pays well but means nothing to you.
- **No recognition** — you do good work and nobody sees it.

Each one has a different fix. Boredom needs a new challenge, not a new career. No recognition needs visibility, not a resignation letter. Most people who feel stuck need one of these four things, not a whole new life. Career researchers describe this as a normal stage of [career development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Career_development) — not a crisis, but a signal that something needs to change.

## Step 2: run a two-week experiment

Do not quit your job to "find yourself". Test a direction in a small, real way first.

- Curious about design? Redesign one page of your company's website in your spare time.
- Thinking about teaching? Run one workshop for your team.
- Drawn to writing? Publish three short pieces and see how it feels.

Two weeks of real doing tells you more than two years of thinking. If the experiment energises you, you have a direction. If it bores you, you have saved yourself a costly mistake.

## Step 3: talk to someone who did it

Find one person who made a move like the one you are considering — not a celebrity, just someone a few steps ahead. Ask them three questions: what surprised you, what was harder than expected, and what would you do differently?

Most people love being asked. One honest conversation can save you a year of guessing.

## Step 4: make a 90-day plan

A vague dream — "someday I will change careers" — never happens. A 90-day plan does. Pick one direction from your experiment and write down:

- **One skill** to build in the next 90 days
- **One project** that proves you can do it
- **One person** to show it to

That is it. Three lines. Review it every Sunday evening for ten minutes. Small plans, repeated, are how careers actually change.

## The feeling is the start, not the end

Feeling stuck is uncomfortable, but it is also useful — it means you have outgrown something, and outgrowing is a sign of growth, not failure. You do not need a dramatic leap. You need a clear diagnosis, a small experiment, one honest conversation, and 90 days.
