The hardest part of a difficult conversation is the first two minutes. After that, you are just talking. Before that, you are standing at the edge of a cold pool, negotiating with yourself.

Our coaches use a simple method for those two minutes. It has three sentences, and it works because it does something most openings do not: it tells the other person what kind of conversation this is going to be.

## Sentence 1: name the topic

"I want to talk about how we divide the work on this project."

One sentence, no blame, no history. You are simply naming the subject, the way you would name a meeting agenda. This matters because the other person's first fear is always the same: *is this an attack?* Naming the topic calmly answers that fear before it grows.

## Sentence 2: say how you feel

"I have been feeling stretched thin, and a bit resentful, to be honest."

Feelings are the most disarming thing you can share, because they cannot be argued with. Nobody can tell you that you do not feel stretched thin. Compare that with "You keep dumping work on me" — which is an accusation, and accusations get a defence, not a conversation.

## Sentence 3: say what you want

"I would like us to agree on who owns what, so it is fair for both of us."

This is the sentence most people skip, and it is the most important one. Without it, the other person hears a complaint. With it, they hear a request — and requests can be answered.

## Then stop talking

This is the hard part. After your three sentences, pause. Let the other person respond. Silence feels awkward for about five seconds, and then it feels respectful — you are giving them room to think, instead of filling the space with justifications.

If they get defensive, do not defend your opening. Repeat the third sentence in different words: "I am not blaming you. I just want us to find a fair split." Calm repetition beats a clever comeback every time.

## Practise it small

Do not save this method for the biggest conversation of your life. Use it on small things first — asking a colleague to turn the music down, telling a friend you would rather meet earlier. Two minutes of honesty, three sentences, and then a pause.

Difficult conversations stay difficult when we rehearse them forever and never start them. Two minutes is all it takes to start. The rest is just talking.
